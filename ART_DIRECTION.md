# Visual direction

## Identity

**Campaign pressroom:** a strategy game presented like a well-made election-night briefing book. The interface should feel authored and tactile, with the confidence of an editorial publication and the energy of a live campaign desk.

The intended materials are dark ink stock, warm paper, faded brass, and sea-glass accents. State and party colors stay strongest on the map, where they carry game information.

## Art treatment

- Use small editorial spot illustrations with a limited print palette, hand-cut silhouettes, restrained halftone, and a little uneven ink registration.
- Favor specific objects and symbols over generic faces, flags, stars-and-stripes, patriotic eagles, glossy 3D, gradient blobs, generic campaign clip-art, and pseudo-text.
- Generated images contain no words, numerals, logos, seals, or recognizable real politicians. UI text and labels remain live text.
- Emoji replacements use named, reusable game-art assets. A legacy emoji may map to a related spot illustration; the emoji itself must not render in the game.
- Keep illustration edges deliberate and readable at small sizes. Avoid distress that hurts contrast or makes the game data harder to scan.

## Interface constraints

- Keep existing component order, grid structure, spacing, dimensions, and responsive breakpoints. Restyle surfaces, colors, typography, focus states, textures, and icon assets without moving UI elements.
- Keep maps, charts, vote totals, and action-state colors legible before adding decoration.
- Use texture as a quiet material cue. Avoid glassy panels, large diffuse glows, and repeating scanline effects.

## Reference study

The Political Process is a useful reference for making state-by-state campaign data scannable; Democracy 4 shows how disciplined color coding can clarify complex systems; 270soft’s President Infinity material emphasizes scenario depth and the strategic map. These are design observations only. Do not reproduce their screens, icons, or artwork.
